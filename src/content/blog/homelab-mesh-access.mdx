The [living-room home server](/blog/homelab-archcraft) has one design flaw:
it assumes I'm in the living room. Music, torrents, Nextcloud, dashboards —
all of it behind a router that doesn't answer to the outside world and was
never going to. Getting the same access from anywhere sounds like a weekend
of following a Tailscale tutorial. From Russia, it turned into a small saga:
CGNAT, deep packet inspection, a config bug that hid for weeks, an ancient
phone, and an Android platform limitation waiting at the end as the final
boss.

## Why the tutorial version doesn't work here

Three constraints, none of them negotiable:

- **Nothing gets in.** Residential connections sit behind CGNAT: no public
  IP, nothing to port-forward, no DDNS trick to save you. Whatever the
  architecture ends up being, the home side has to *dial out* to something
  reachable.
- **Plain VPNs don't get out.** Russia's traffic-inspection hardware (TSPU)
  fingerprints and drops OpenVPN and WireGuard handshakes on residential
  lines. A bare WireGuard link from home mostly never forms at all.
- **Tailscale's SaaS is shaped wrong for this.** Its coordination server and
  public DERP relays are unreliable to reach from here, and signing up
  requires a foreign SSO login — which requires a working VPN first.
  Circular.

So: self-host the control plane. **headscale** reimplements Tailscale's
coordination server, and stock Tailscale clients will happily point at it
via `--login-server`. Mine went onto a small VPS in Poland I already run —
the same box that hosts a DPI-bypass proxy tier (Hysteria2/Reality family,
which will matter later) and has therefore already proven, daily, that HTTPS
from my ISP to it survives. Every device — home server, laptops, phone —
dials *out* to that VPS over HTTPS. CGNAT never comes up, and to the
inspection boxes along the way it doesn't look much like a VPN.

## Weeks of it working, until it didn't

Enrolling the home server and a laptop went smoothly, and the mesh ran fine —
for weeks. Then a laptop roamed onto a different network and the whole thing
just... stopped. No error, no useful timeout. The home server was simply
gone.

The cause was dumb in the specific way these things always are: the
self-hosted DERP relay — the fallback that carries traffic when two peers
can't connect directly — still had the **example placeholder addresses**
from the documentation in its config. `198.51.100.1` and `2001:db8::1` are
the RFC 5737 / RFC 3849 ranges, reserved precisely so that no real traffic
ever goes to them. Every client dutifully dialed an unroutable address for
its relay fallback, failed silently, and fell back to nothing.

What made it invisible: on the same LAN, WireGuard peers negotiate a
**direct** connection and never touch the relay. The bug only lives in the
gap between "both devices are online" and "both devices can see each other
directly" — which is almost every situation *except* two machines sharing a
home network. The mesh had been broken from day one, and home was the one
place that couldn't reveal it.

The lesson, written down because I will forget it: **"works at home,
unreachable everywhere else" is a relay problem, not a device problem.**
`tailscale netcheck` — does `UDP` report true, what's the `Nearest DERP` —
plus a sanity check that the relay's advertised IP is one it's actually
reachable at, catches this in a minute. Noticing it organically took weeks.

## The 32-bit phone

With the relay fixed, the laptops were done. The phone was not, because the
phone is a 32-bit **armv7** device, and every sensible-looking path died in
sequence:

- Termux's `tailscale` package lives in the TUR repo, which builds
  **aarch64 only** — `unable to locate package`.
- A NetBird-branded app that looked promising wanted a "management server
  URL" and rejected headscale outright. Different coordination protocol;
  the resemblance was only in the name.
- The official static Tailscale binary for ARM isn't a PIE build, and
  modern Android flatly refuses to execute it.
- A custom Bionic-targeted build got the furthest, then died on `TLS
  segment underaligned`: Android's C library demands stricter alignment
  than a stock Go cross-compile emits, and fixing that collides with Go's
  hard-coded float ABI for 32-bit ARM. Known dead end. Do not retry.

What worked felt less like a fix than a loophole. The **official** Tailscale
app runs fine on armv7 — it just hides its "custom coordination server"
setting until you've logged in once. And logging in means reaching
`login.tailscale.com`, which is blocked from here. So: DPI-bypass tunnel on,
sign into a free Tailscale SaaS account, and the hidden **"use an alternate
server"** option appears. Point it at headscale, approve the registration
server-side, tunnel back off. Whole-phone mesh VPN on hardware that every
"just install Tailscale" guide quietly assumes was recycled years ago.

## Mounts and names that assumed the LAN

The mesh now reached everything; not everything was ready to be reached. The
home server's drives are NFS mounts on the laptop — automounted lazily on
first access, a genuinely nice setup — except the mounts pointed at a
hostname that only resolves on the home LAN. Anywhere else, touching the
path meant hanging until the timeout.

The fix was two sets of mountpoints, not one clever one:

- The LAN mounts, unchanged: full speed, no encryption overhead.
- A parallel set aimed at the mesh address, for when the laptop is away,
  with the mount timeout bumped to survive internet latency instead of LAN
  latency.

I specifically didn't want a single mountpoint that auto-detects the
network. That's real machinery — reachability probes, a remount-on-change
service — to avoid typing a different path a few times a month, and it
would shove bulk file transfers through an encrypted tunnel while I sit
three meters from the server. Two names, each doing one obvious thing.

The web dashboards (Navidrome, the torrent UI, the rest) had the same
which-name-resolves-where problem plus one extra constraint: their TLS
certificate is a wildcard covering exactly one hostname level, so the NFS
trick — a parallel mesh-only name — would make every browser complain about
a mismatch. For these, the answer flipped: route them through the mesh
*unconditionally*. A dashboard is a few kilobytes of HTML; the encryption
tax is nothing, and when both ends are on the same LAN the mesh negotiates
a direct local-speed path anyway.

## One phone, one VPN slot

Which leaves the problem that had been sitting there since the beginning,
unnamed: **Android lets exactly one app hold the VPN interface.** Turn on
Tailscale and the DPI-bypass tunnel dies. Turn the tunnel back on and
Tailscale dies. Neither app is wrong — `VpnService` works this way, full
stop.

For a while the answer was to toggle. It was fine, in the way things are
fine right up until they aren't: the moment I wanted normal browsing *and*
a quick look at the home server in the same session, "fine" ended.

The fix was obvious the second it clicked: **the phone doesn't need to be
in the mesh if the thing it's already tunneling through is.** The VPS
terminating the DPI-bypass tunnel had never joined its own mesh — it was
purely an internet relay. Enroll it like any other device and it gets a
mesh address of its own; from that moment its proxy's outbound connections,
never restricted to begin with, reach the home server's mesh address like
any other destination. No forwarding rules, no NAT tricks. A mesh peer
doesn't need to be told how to route to the mesh.

So the phone now runs one VPN app, permanently, for everything. On the rare
occasion it needs the home server, the path is already there — inside the
tunnel it was going to use anyway.

## The one remaining rough spot

The friendly mesh-wide hostnames (the `.lab` names the dashboards answer
to) come from MagicDNS, which headscale pushes to registered mesh
*clients* — and the phone, by design, no longer is one. So from the phone
it's a raw mesh IP for the couple of services that need one. I'll take that
trade over fighting a VPN toggle several times a day.

Everything else on the list — the placeholder relay, the armv7 gauntlet,
the split mounts — turned out to be a one-time cost. This is the first
piece of the homelab that feels finished: not because nothing's left to
improve, but because nothing's left that interrupts.
